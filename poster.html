STATS 401 · Final project · CS201 homework process

The score has a past

Homework scores cluster near full marks. We followed online-judge submissions to see how differently students get there, and whether the path travels with the exam.

Zaozao Wang data acquisition, cleaning, analysisZhengxiang Liu visualization design, D3 implementation

Watch it move The playable data film, all twelve figures, tables and methods

Why it matters

In of problem slots, every student who attempted the problem reached full marks in the end. What the 100 does not show is the first try, the retries, or the hour they arrived.

The online judge keeps that process in its submission log. Reading it helps students plan their own work, and helps TAs and instructors see where and when support is needed.

Questions

  1. When does homework work happen, and is a busy hour many students or a few students submitting again and again?Fig 1
  2. Do retries pay off, and does that depend on the homework, the time since the last try, or the point in the window?Fig 2 · 3
  3. What does a final 100 hide about how students got there?Fig 4
  4. Does the way students submit relate to their midterm score?Fig 5

Data & method

  • SourceCS201 online judge, read-only snapshot of : course submissions from a -student roster; inside homework windows ( submitters, problem slots).
  • CleaningTest account removed; each submission kept only inside its own homework window; scores scaled to 0–100 per problem; judge errors skipped.
  • AnalysisFirst try, best within three, best observed; eligible retries; four-hour windows; five phases per homework. Exam: grades linked privately; Spearman ρ, 5,000-draw bootstrap.
  • PrivacyOnly aggregates are published; any group of fewer than five students is withheld.
  • Built withPython for cleaning; D3 v7, SVG and Canvas for the film and this poster. Online: switch views, sort, highlight a homework, follow a problem across figures, hover for counts, scrub the story.

3When in the window?Film · Figure 12

Retries progress unevenly from opening to deadline, and each homework has its own rhythm.

How to readEach row is a homework. Its open period, from release to deadline, is cut into five equal parts: 0–20% is the first fifth, 80–100% the last. A cell is the share of that part's eligible retries that set a new best (darker is higher), with the counts and S, the number of students.

What we seeVisible rates run from % to %, and no single pattern repeats across the homeworks.

CarefulHatched cells have fewer than five students: withheld, not zero. A phase lasts hours depending on the homework. Deadlines are the configured ones; individual extensions are unknown. No deadline effect is estimated.

5Does rushing show in the exam?Film · Figure 06

Last-minute rushing went with lower midterm scores.

How to readEach row is one correlation across the same students, each with one value for the behaviour and one midterm score; no one is split into groups. ρ (rho) compares the two rankings: −1 opposite order, 0 no relation, +1 same order. A bar that crosses 0 leaves the direction unclear.

What we seeRushing, the share of a student's submissions sent in the last 24 hours before each deadline, has ρ = (95% interval to ): the only bar entirely below 0. Leaving out any one student keeps it between and .

Careful"Rushing" names submission timing, not effort or study time. Grades were supplied for of eligible students: a partial, non-random sample, using pre-exam only. An association in a small group, not a cause and not a prediction.

Read with care

  • Observational data from one course: associations, not causes or predictions.
  • Submissions are not effort; time between them is not study time.
  • Groups of fewer than five students are withheld, never shown as zero.

What the 100 was hiding

  1. One score, many roads.Figures 1 · 4Full marks hide very different paths: busy windows and a few students submitting again and again; problems solved at once and problems still short after three tries.
  2. Retries progress unevenly.Figures 2 · 3% of eligible retries set a new best, at rates that differ by homework, by time since the last attempt and by phase of the window.
  3. Rushing went with lower scores.Figure 5Among supplied grades, more last-minute submitting went with lower midterm scores (ρ ): an association, not a cause.
